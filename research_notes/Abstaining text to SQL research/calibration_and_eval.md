# Calibration Methods with Statistical Guarantees, and Evaluation Protocols, for Selective Prediction in LLMs (as of 2026-11-04)

Context for these notes: a small text-to-SQL model trained with RL to answer or abstain. After training, a calibration step on held-out data should yield a guarantee of the form "error <= alpha on the queries the model answers." Each section below answers one key question. Verification level is marked per finding: items cited to a fetched arXiv page or full text are verified at that level; items from search snippets are marked as such; anything not confirmable is marked UNVERIFIED.

A note on reading these notes: "calibration" here means a post-training step that picks a confidence cutoff on held-out data so a stated error target provably holds; "coverage" means the fraction of queries the model chooses to answer; "selective risk" means the error rate counted only on the answered queries.

## Key Question 1: Conformal risk control (CRC) — what it guarantees, what it assumes, how it applies to selective prediction/abstention

### Takeaway
CRC (Angelopoulos, Bates, Fisch, Lei, Schuster, arXiv 2208.02814 — verified, full HTML fetched) picks a single threshold lambda-hat on n exchangeable calibration points so that the *expected value* of any bounded, monotone (non-increasing in lambda) loss on a fresh test point is at most alpha. The guarantee is marginal (it holds on average over draws of the calibration set, not for your one specific calibration set), distribution-free, and exact up to an O(1/n) term. It directly fits abstention when the loss is framed per-query (e.g., "answered AND wrong"), but the guarantee "error <= alpha *conditional on answering*" is a ratio of expectations, which plain CRC does not bound directly — the 2024-2026 conformal-abstention papers handle that conditional form with extra machinery.

### Cited Findings
- Exact guarantee (Theorem 1): E[ l(C_lambda-hat(X_{n+1}), Y_{n+1}) ] <= alpha, i.e., the expected loss at the calibrated threshold is at most the target alpha — [Conformal Risk Control, arXiv 2208.02814 (HTML v4)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2208.02814)
- Conditions for the guarantee: the per-point losses L_i(lambda) must be (a) non-increasing in lambda, (b) right-continuous, (c) bounded above by a known constant B, (d) satisfy L_i(lambda_max) <= alpha, and (e) the calibration points and the test point must be exchangeable (any ordering equally likely — i.i.d. data is the standard case) — [arXiv 2208.02814 (HTML v4)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2208.02814)
- Threshold selection rule: lambda-hat = inf{ lambda : (n/(n+1)) * R-hat_n(lambda) + B/(n+1) <= alpha }, where R-hat_n is the average calibration loss; the B/(n+1) inflation term accounts for the unseen test point. If no lambda qualifies, lambda-hat = lambda_max — [arXiv 2208.02814 (HTML v4)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2208.02814)
- Tightness (Theorem 2): under mild extra conditions (i.i.d., no atoms), E[L_{n+1}(lambda-hat)] >= alpha - 2B/(n+1), so the method is not meaningfully conservative; the authors "do not know whether this factor of 2 is improvable" — [arXiv 2208.02814 (HTML v4)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2208.02814)
- CRC "generalizes split conformal prediction together with its coverage guarantee" (coverage is the special case where the loss is the 0/1 miscoverage indicator) — [arXiv 2208.02814 abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2208.02814)
- The guarantee is marginal: the expectation is over all n+1 points including the calibration draw; it holds on average over calibration sets, not conditionally on the realized one. High-probability / quantile risk control is treated separately in the paper's Section 4.2 — [arXiv 2208.02814 (HTML v4)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2208.02814)
- CRC does NOT control non-monotone risks in general (Proposition 1); a monotonization workaround exists but with only asymptotic guarantees in one variant — [arXiv 2208.02814 (HTML v4)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2208.02814)
- The paper's worked examples are false-negative-rate control in tumor segmentation (n=1000, alpha=0.1, realized mean risk 0.0987) and MS-COCO multilabel (n=4000, alpha=0.1, realized 0.0996), plus hierarchical ImageNet and open-domain-QA F1 control; there is no abstention example in the paper itself — [arXiv 2208.02814 (HTML v4)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2208.02814)
- Paper status: arXiv v4 revised 13 Jun 2025; stat.ME preprint page does not name a venue (the paper is widely cited as ICLR 2024 — UNVERIFIED from the page) — [arXiv 2208.02814](https://arxiv.org/abs/2208.02814)

LLM applications of conformal methods to abstention/generation (2024-2026):
- **Conformal Abstention** (Yadkori, Kuzborskij, Stutz, Gyorgy, Fisch, Doucet, et al., arXiv 2405.01563, Apr 2024): uses the LLM to self-evaluate similarity among k sampled responses (a self-consistency score), then calibrates an abstention rule with conformal prediction, with "rigorous theoretical guarantees on the hallucination rate (error rate)". On long-form Temporal Sequences it abstains "significantly less" than log-probability baselines; on TriviaQA it is comparable — [arXiv 2405.01563](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.01563). (Whether the bound is on error-given-answered vs. the joint event was not confirmable from the abstract page — see Gaps.)
- **Conformal Language Modeling** (Quach, Fisch, Schuster, Yala, Sohn, Jaakkola, Barzilay, ICLR 2024, arXiv 2306.10193): samples candidates with a calibrated stopping rule and a simultaneously calibrated rejection rule; proves the returned set "contains at least one acceptable answer with high probability" — [arXiv 2306.10193](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.10193)
- **Conformal Factuality** (Mohri & Hashimoto, arXiv 2402.10978, Feb 2024): conformal prediction as a back-off algorithm that makes LM outputs progressively less specific until correct; "80-90% correctness guarantees while retaining the majority of the LM's original output" on FActScore/NaturalQuestions/MATH — [arXiv 2402.10978](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.10978)
- **Learning Conformal Abstention Policies** (arXiv 2502.06884, Feb 2025): treats conformal thresholds as actions tuned by an RL policy; reports ~90% coverage maintained and 22.19% AUROC gains over LAC/APS baselines for hallucination detection (search snippet level, full paper not fetched) — [arXiv 2502.06884](https://www.arxiv.org/abs/2502.06884)
- **When Can Conformal Risk Control Certify LLM Outputs?** (arXiv 2606.29054, 2026): bounds, impossibility results, and adaptations for CRC over structured generation; includes a fixed-sequence testing procedure that does not require the risk to be monotone in the threshold (search snippet level) — [arXiv 2606.29054](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.29054)
- **SAFER: Risk-Constrained Sample-then-Filter** (arXiv 2510.10193, listed in search as ICLR 2026): combines Learn-then-Test for a sampling budget with CRC for filtering, and abstains outright when the calibration step shows the risk bound cannot be met (search snippet level) — [arXiv 2510.10193](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.10193v3)
- **Text-to-SQL specific**: "Certified Against Which Oracle? Execution Labels Set the Reported Risk of Conformal Abstention for Text-to-SQL" (arXiv 2609.25938, 2026) argues the correctness labels used to calibrate conformal abstention depend on the benchmark's database, and a single lenient database can inflate reported reliability (search snippet level) — [arXiv 2609.25938](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.25938)

### Inferences
- For the abstain-SQL use case, the cleanest CRC formulation is: let lambda be a confidence cutoff, answer when confidence >= lambda, and define the per-query loss L_i(lambda) = 1{model answers query i at cutoff lambda AND the SQL is wrong}. Raising the cutoff can only remove answered queries, so the loss is non-increasing in the "abstain more" direction, bounded by B=1 — exactly CRC's setting. This controls E[answered AND wrong] = the *unconditional* rate of confidently-wrong answers, sometimes called the abstaining risk.
- The user-facing target "error <= alpha among answered queries" (selective risk, a conditional rate) is a ratio E[wrong AND answered]/E[answered] and is NOT monotone-bounded in the simple CRC sense; controlling it needs either (a) the joint-event bound above plus a separately reported coverage, (b) a ratio-aware test as in Learn-then-Test (Question 2), or (c) the constructions in the conformal-abstention papers. This distinction matters for how the guarantee is worded in a paper or README.
- The marginal nature of the guarantee means: over many hypothetical re-draws of the calibration set, the average test risk is <= alpha; any single calibration set can land above alpha. With B=1 and small n (e.g., n=500), the B/(n+1) inflation costs ~0.002 of the alpha budget — cheap — but calibration-set-to-calibration-set spread is the real variance source and should be reported (see Question 6 on bootstrap).
- The 2026 text-to-SQL critique (execution labels) implies the guarantee is only as good as the correctness oracle: if "correct" is judged by execution match on one database instance, the certified risk is relative to that oracle, not to true semantic equivalence. An abstain-SQL paper should state its oracle explicitly.

## Key Question 2: Learn-then-Test (LTT) — how it differs, when it is needed instead of CRC

### Takeaway
LTT (Angelopoulos, Bates, Candes, Jordan, Lei, arXiv 2110.01052 — verified at abstract level) reframes threshold/hyperparameter selection as multiple hypothesis testing: for each candidate configuration it tests "risk > alpha" and keeps only configurations where that hypothesis is rejected, giving a distribution-free, finite-sample, *high-probability* guarantee (holds with probability >= 1-delta over the calibration draw, not just on average). Use LTT instead of CRC when any of these hold: the risk is not monotone in the knob; there are several knobs calibrated jointly; the target is a ratio/conditional risk; or a high-probability (PAC-style) statement is wanted rather than an in-expectation one.

### Cited Findings
- LTT is "a framework for calibrating machine learning models so that their predictions satisfy explicit, finite-sample statistical guarantees", works "with any underlying model and (unknown) data-generating distribution", and does "not require model refitting"; the "main insight is to reframe the risk-control problem as multiple hypothesis testing" — [Learn then Test, arXiv 2110.01052 abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.01052)
- Demonstrated risks go beyond coverage: false-discovery-rate control in multi-label classification, intersection-over-union control in segmentation, and *joint* control of outlier-detection type-1 error together with coverage — i.e., simultaneous multi-risk, multi-parameter calibration — [arXiv 2110.01052 abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.01052)
- The CRC paper itself classifies LTT among "high-probability risk control" methods and contrasts them with CRC's in-expectation guarantee; it also proves CRC does not handle non-monotone risks in general (Proposition 1) — [arXiv 2208.02814 (HTML v4)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2208.02814)
- Concrete LTT recipe as used in a 2026 LLM paper (IntroConformal): compute, for each candidate threshold, a Hoeffding upper confidence bound on the empirical risk (Hoeffding = a standard concentration bound: how far a sample average can sit from the true average); pick the least conservative threshold whose bound is <= alpha; apply a Bonferroni correction (divide the error budget across all thresholds tested) so all tests are simultaneously valid — at per-test alpha = delta = 0.1 this yielded concurrent control at ~0.170 (search snippet level) — [IntroConformal, arXiv 2609.01375](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.01375)
- Conformal Language Modeling calibrates a stopping rule and a rejection rule *simultaneously* and gives a high-probability guarantee ("contains at least one acceptable answer with high probability") — the canonical LLM case where multiple interacting knobs pushed the authors beyond single-threshold CRC — [arXiv 2306.10193](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.10193)
- SAFER (2026) uses LTT to certify a sampling budget and CRC for filtering, and abstains when no configuration passes the test — an example of composing the two (search snippet level) — [arXiv 2510.10193](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.10193v3)
- LTT venue: arXiv v5 (Sep 2022); page names no journal (commonly cited as Annals of Statistics 2025 / journal publication — UNVERIFIED) — [arXiv 2110.01052](https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.01052)

### Inferences
- Decision rule for the abstain-SQL project: if the calibrated knob is one confidence cutoff and the controlled quantity is the unconditional confidently-wrong rate (monotone in the cutoff), CRC is simpler and tighter. If the stated guarantee must be "with probability >= 1-delta over the calibration draw, selective risk <= alpha" — i.e., a conditional rate with a confidence level, the form most readers will assume from the phrase "error <= alpha on answered queries" — LTT (or the closely related SGR bound in Question 3) is the right tool, because a per-threshold binomial/Hoeffding test can be run on the *answered subset* at each threshold.
- LTT's cost relative to CRC: the multiple-testing correction and the concentration bound make it more conservative at the same n (it answers less, or needs larger n for the same alpha); CRC's in-expectation guarantee is tight up to 2B/(n+1) but is a weaker statement. This trade should be stated explicitly when comparing methods.
- For a ratio target like selective risk, a clean LTT instantiation: fix a grid of cutoffs; at each cutoff, the answered calibration points' errors are Bernoulli draws, so an exact binomial tail p-value for H0: conditional error > alpha is available; use fixed-sequence testing (walk the grid from most-conservative to least, stop at first failure) to avoid Bonferroni's full penalty — fixed-sequence validity without monotonicity is noted in the 2026 CRC-certification paper (snippet level).

### Gaps
- The exact LTT procedure details (p-value construction, family-wise error rate control variants, fixed-sequence vs. Bonferroni) are in the full paper, which was not fetched; the mechanism summary above beyond the abstract is corroborated only by the IntroConformal snippet. Treat specifics as to-verify-from-primary before quoting in a paper.

## Key Question 3: Simple threshold selection on a calibration split — what guarantee it gives; Geifman & El-Yaniv framework

### Takeaway
Picking the cutoff where empirical selective risk on a calibration split equals alpha gives *no* finite-sample guarantee — roughly, the test risk lands above alpha about half the time when the empirical estimate sits exactly at the boundary. Geifman & El-Yaniv's SGR (Selection with Guaranteed Risk, NeurIPS-era 2017, arXiv 1705.08500 — verified at abstract level) is the classical fix: it chooses the cutoff using a tail bound on the binomial error count among answered calibration points, yielding "risk <= r* with probability >= 1-delta". SGR is effectively a special case of the LTT philosophy, published four years earlier, for the single-threshold selective-classification problem.

### Cited Findings
- Geifman & El-Yaniv 2017: given a trained classifier and a user-set desired risk level, the method constructs a selective classifier that "rejects instances as needed, to grant the desired risk (with high probability)"; headline result: on ImageNet top-5, "a 2% error can be guaranteed with 99.9% probability, with almost 60% test coverage" — [Selective Classification for Deep Neural Networks, arXiv 1705.08500 abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/1705.08500)
- The selective-prediction framework from that line of work defines two quantities: coverage = the fraction of inputs the model answers, and selective risk = the average loss counted only over answered inputs (formal definitions are in the full paper and in El-Yaniv & Wiener's earlier JMLR work; the fetched abstract page states the trade-off informally) — [arXiv 1705.08500](https://arxiv.org/abs/1705.08500)
- A 2026 text-to-SQL selective-prediction study used exactly the simple protocol — thresholds picked on a calibration split, then evaluated on a held-out split — and reported the resulting risk/coverage frontier rather than a certified bound (e.g., the best signal "answers 27% of questions at 24% selective risk") — [What Predicts Correctness in Text-to-SQL?, arXiv 2607.06799](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.06799)
- CRC's own construction shows what the naive method is missing: the (n/(n+1)) R-hat + B/(n+1) <= alpha inflation is precisely the correction that turns empirical threshold-matching into a guarantee in expectation — [arXiv 2208.02814 (HTML v4)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2208.02814)

### Inferences
- Hierarchy worth stating in the project write-up, weakest to strongest: (1) naive thresholding — empirical risk <= alpha on the calibration split; only an asymptotic/large-n heuristic, test risk exceeds alpha with non-trivial probability at finite n; (2) CRC — expected test risk <= alpha, exactly, for monotone unconditional risks, with O(1/n) tightness; (3) SGR / LTT — test risk <= alpha with probability >= 1-delta over the calibration draw, applicable to conditional (selective) risk, at the price of conservatism that grows as delta shrinks and n shrinks.
- The SGR internal mechanics (binomial tail inversion, often attributed to a Gascuel-Caraux-style bound, applied with a Bonferroni/geometric grid over thresholds) are consistent with the abstract's "with high probability" claim but were not verifiable from the fetched page — treat the mechanism description as from-memory, UNVERIFIED at quote level.
- Practical sizing intuition: with alpha = 0.05 and delta = 0.05, a binomial tail bound needs on the order of several hundred *answered* calibration points before the certified threshold stops being much more conservative than the empirical one; since only answered points count, low-coverage models need disproportionately large calibration splits. (Inference from standard binomial concentration, not a cited result.)

### Gaps
- The formal SGR algorithm, its exact bound, and the numerical-search procedure were not retrievable this session (abstract page only; the PDF was not fetched). Fetch arXiv 1705.08500 full text before quoting the algorithm.
- El-Yaniv & Wiener (JMLR 2010), the origin of the selective risk/coverage formalism, was not fetched; definitions above marked accordingly.

## Key Question 4: Which confidence signal to calibrate for LLM selective prediction (code/SQL)

### Takeaway
For text-to-SQL specifically, the published evidence is consistent: raw signals are mediocre and overconfident; the model's full-sequence probability (the product of token probabilities over the whole query) is a surprisingly strong, cheap baseline that beats asking the model to self-check or verbalize confidence; execution-based self-consistency (sample k, compare execution results) is a strong black-box signal but has a hard ceiling; LLM-judge/verifier signals and especially cross-provider ensembles rank best (AUROC ~0.78-0.82) and calibrate well after rescaling — but fine-tuned verifiers lose most of their edge on unseen schemas.

### Cited Findings
- Full-sequence probability "outperforms recent methods that rely on follow-up prompts for self-checking and confidence verbalization" for SQL confidence, across two benchmarks and multiple LLM architectures; the paper's contribution is rescaling (recalibrating) those probabilities — [Ramachandran & Sarawagi, arXiv 2411.16742 abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.16742)
- In the 2026 selective-prediction study (single author R. Richardson): black-box signals cluster at 0.61-0.68 AUROC (AUROC = probability the signal ranks a random correct query above a random wrong one; 0.5 is coin-flip): string self-consistency 0.675, log-probability ~0.67, schema-relevance and executability similar; LLM-judge verifiers 0.72 (GPT-4o-mini) to 0.78 (Claude); a two-provider judge ensemble reaches 0.82 AUROC with expected calibration error 0.03 (ECE = average gap between stated confidence and actual accuracy; 0.03 is well calibrated); raw self-consistency "offers no valid low-risk subset" — [arXiv 2607.06799](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.06799)
- Same study: fine-tuned encoder and generative verifiers hit ~0.77-0.79 AUROC in-distribution but drop to ~0.66 on unseen schemas, and scaling to 7B, schema diversity, distillation, and cross-benchmark training did not close the gap — [arXiv 2607.06799](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.06799)
- A 2025 benchmark of black-box and white-box confidence estimation on Spider and BIRD proposes a syntax-aware logit method (weighting token logits by SQL-structural role) and reports up to 16% ECE reduction over baselines; it also notes prior abstention work (TriageSQL, TrustSQL) provided benchmarks but not direct confidence scoring (search snippet level) — [arXiv 2508.14056](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.14056)
- For long-form generation, a self-consistency-style match score calibrated conformally abstained much less than log-probability baselines at the same hallucination-rate target (Temporal Sequences), and matched them on short-answer TriviaQA — [Yadkori et al., arXiv 2405.01563](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.01563)
- TrustSQL's own comparison of abstention mechanisms: among open-source unified models, T5 with MaxEnt / MaxProb uncertainty (maximum-entropy / maximum-probability scores from the model's own output distribution) performed best across penalty levels; among API methods, a 5-sample agreement vote (SQLPrompt[Voting] — abstain unless all five samples agree) was "notably strong across penalties given its simplicity"; pipeline variants with GPT-4o infeasibility and error detectors led at high penalties — [TrustSQL, arXiv 2403.15879 (HTML v6)](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.15879v6)
- Earlier error-detection work for text-to-SQL used entropy-based selective classifiers and studied the coverage-risk trade-off (search snippet level) — [Somov & Tutubalina, arXiv 2501.09527](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.09527)
- In image classification, a large ICLR 2023 (oral) evaluation found "a simple softmax response baseline as the overall best performing method" for failure detection — the same "cheap signal is hard to beat" pattern — [Jaeger et al., arXiv 2211.15259 abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.15259)

### Inferences
- For the RL abstain-SQL model, three candidate signals map onto this evidence: (1) the model's own learned abstain decision — a binary action, not a score, so it cannot be threshold-calibrated by itself; (2) sequence log-probability (or the abstain-token probability) — cheap, white-box, and per the Ramachandran & Sarawagi result the best single signal to rescale; (3) k-sample execution agreement — stronger black-box ranking signal, k-times the cost, and the signal used by the strongest conformal-abstention paper (Yadkori). A sensible design: calibrate the threshold on signal (2) or (3) *on top of* the RL policy, so the RL abstain action provides base selectivity and the conformal/LTT layer provides the certificate.
- Published evidence specifically comparing "RL-trained abstention head/action" against logprob or self-consistency calibration for SQL was not found; the closest is TrustSQL's unified-vs-pipeline comparison. This is a genuine open slot the project's experiments could fill.
- Execution-based self-consistency has a label-oracle entanglement (same issue as Question 1's execution-label critique): two wrong queries can agree on a lenient database instance, so agreement overstates correctness exactly where the eval oracle is weak.

### Gaps
- No paper was found that directly evaluates calibrating an RL-trained abstain policy's own decision signal (e.g., abstain-action logit) for text-to-SQL; evidence above is for logprobs, self-consistency, verbalized confidence, and external verifiers.
- ECE numbers for individual signals (logprob, self-consistency) in the Richardson study were not visible from the abstract; the overconfidence claim for raw self-consistency and verifier probabilities comes from the earlier search snippet of that paper, not the fetched abstract. ## Key Question 5: Distribution shift — what breaks, measured degradation, and a calibrate-on-A/test-on-B design

### Takeaway
Exchangeability between calibration and test data is the *entire* assumption behind conformal/CRC guarantees, and a new schema family is a covariate shift that voids the certificate silently — the method still outputs a threshold, it just no longer means anything. Two repair lines exist: weighted conformal prediction (Tibshirani et al. 2019) when the calibration-to-test likelihood ratio is known or estimable, and nonexchangeable conformal (Barber et al. 2022) which bounds how much coverage can degrade. Empirically for SQL, the best-measured proxy is the verifier-generalization drop (AUROC ~0.78 to ~0.66 on unseen schemas) — the *ranking signal itself* degrades across schema families, which compounds the calibration problem.

### Cited Findings
- Standard conformal validity requires exchangeability; under covariate shift (the input distribution P(X) changes but P(Y|X) does not), a weighted version of conformal prediction restores distribution-free intervals, where the weights are the likelihood ratio between test and training covariate distributions; the ratio must be known or "estimated accurately with access to a large set of unlabeled data (test covariate points)" — [Conformal Prediction Under Covariate Shift, arXiv 1904.06019 abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/1904.06019)
- Barber, Candes, Ramdas, Tibshirani 2022 generalize conformal prediction beyond exchangeability using weighted quantiles "to introduce robustness against distribution drift", with "substantially less loss of coverage when exchangeability is violated" and unchanged guarantees when data are exchangeable — [Conformal prediction beyond exchangeability, arXiv 2202.13415 abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2202.13415). The paper's known quantitative result — coverage gap bounded by a weighted sum of total-variation distances between calibration points and the test point — was NOT confirmable from the abstract page; UNVERIFIED at quote level, verify from the full PDF before citing numerically.
- Measured signal degradation across schema families in text-to-SQL: fine-tuned verifiers at ~0.77-0.79 AUROC in-distribution fall to ~0.66 on unseen schemas, and scale/diversity/distillation interventions did not close the gap — [arXiv 2607.06799](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.06799)
- The correctness oracle itself shifts across databases: reported risk of conformal abstention for text-to-SQL depends on which execution labels/database instances define "correct", and a single lenient database inflates reported reliability (search snippet level) — [arXiv 2609.25938](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.25938)
- A 2026 paper studies when CRC can certify structured LLM generation at all, with impossibility results and adaptations (search snippet level) — [arXiv 2606.29054](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.29054)
- TrustSQL spans four datasets — ATIS, Advising, EHRSQL, Spider — i.e., three single-domain schema families plus one cross-domain set, a ready-made shift grid — [arXiv 2403.15879 (HTML v6)](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.15879v6)

### Inferences
- Mechanism of breakage, in plain terms: the CRC threshold is the (inflated) empirical quantile of calibration losses; under shift the test losses are drawn from a different distribution, so the quantile no longer transfers. Nothing in the procedure detects this — realized risk can exceed alpha by an arbitrary amount depending on shift size. Formally the excess is controlled by how statistically distinguishable the two distributions are (the Barber et al. line), which for disjoint schema families can be large.
- Proposed experiment design (calibrate on schema family A, test on family B), grounded in the sources above: (1) partition by database/schema family (Spider's split, or TrustSQL's four datasets) so calibration and test never share a schema; (2) calibrate lambda-hat at several alphas on family A; (3) on family B report realized selective risk, realized coverage, and the gap (realized risk minus alpha) with item-level bootstrap intervals; (4) include the i.i.d. control — calibrate and test on disjoint halves of the *same* family — to separate finite-sample noise from shift; (5) repeat over all A-to-B ordered pairs to get a gap matrix; (6) optionally test the repair: weighted conformal with a likelihood ratio estimated from unlabeled family-B questions (e.g., a domain classifier on question/schema embeddings), per Tibshirani et al. (Steps 1-5 are an inference assembling standard pieces; no paper doing exactly this for text-to-SQL abstention was found — the 2609.25938 paper is the nearest neighbor and varies the *oracle*, not the calibration domain.)
- Expected qualitative outcome, from the verifier-generalization finding: both the guarantee gap and the usable coverage should worsen on unseen schema families, since the confidence signal's ranking power itself drops ~0.12 AUROC.

### Gaps
- No published measurement of conformal/CRC guarantee degradation specifically across text-to-SQL schema families was found (searches surfaced the oracle-dependence paper, not a calibration-transfer study). The experiment above would be novel as far as this search could determine.
- The TV-distance coverage-gap bound of Barber et al.: exact statement UNVERIFIED this session (abstract page only).

## Key Question 6: Metrics and statistical practice for risk-coverage evaluation

### Takeaway
The standard kit: coverage (fraction answered), selective risk (error among answered), the risk-coverage curve traced by sweeping the threshold, and AURC (area under that curve — equivalently, the average selective risk across coverage levels; lower is better, and it scores the ranking quality of the confidence signal independent of any single threshold). TrustSQL adds a penalty-based Reliability Score RS(c) that makes wrong answers cost c times a correct one — the natural deployment-weighted headline metric for abstaining text-to-SQL. For uncertainty over few RL seeds, follow the NeurIPS-2021 Outstanding Paper playbook: report interval estimates (bootstrap confidence intervals — re-sampling your own results many times to see how much the number wobbles), prefer the interquartile mean (the mean of the middle 50% of runs, which ignores outlier seeds) over the plain mean, and show performance profiles; the rliable library implements all of it.

### Cited Findings
- Selective classification trades coverage for risk: the user sets a desired risk and the classifier rejects as needed to grant it with high probability (coverage = fraction answered; selective risk = error on the answered fraction; formal definitions in the full paper) — [Geifman & El-Yaniv, arXiv 1705.08500 abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/1705.08500)
- AURC provenance: the metric is standardly attributed to Geifman, Uziel & El-Yaniv (ICLR 2019, arXiv 1805.08206); the fetched abstract describes the paper's uncertainty-estimation focus but does not itself state the AURC definition, and the Jaeger et al. (ICLR 2023 oral) abstract likewise does not name AURC — the precise definitional sentences live in the full texts. Definition as used in the field: sweep the confidence threshold from answer-everything down to answer-nothing, plot selective risk against coverage, and integrate; excess AURC (E-AURC) subtracts the AURC of a hypothetical perfect ranking. Marked: definition text UNVERIFIED from fetched pages — [arXiv 1805.08206](https://arxiv.org/abs/1805.08206); [arXiv 2211.15259](https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.15259)
- TrustSQL Reliability Score, verified from the full HTML: per-sample score phi_c(x) = +1 for correct SQL on a feasible question; 0 for abstaining on a feasible question; -c for incorrect SQL on a feasible question; -c for answering an infeasible question at all; +1 for abstaining on an infeasible question; the aggregate RS is the mean of phi_c. Reported at c = 0, c = 10, and c = N (N = number of evaluated samples, i.e., one confidently-wrong answer can wipe out a perfect score on everything else). RS(0) equals plain execution accuracy when only feasible questions are evaluated — [TrustSQL, arXiv 2403.15879 (HTML v6)](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.15879v6)
- TrustSQL findings that shape evaluation design: scores good at c=0 do not carry to c=N (many methods go negative); abstaining on everything scores RS = 50 on their 50/50 feasible/infeasible test sets, a floor any useful model must beat; test sets contain equal numbers of feasible and infeasible questions; infeasible questions come in five types (column-surface, column-related, column-unrelated, non-sql, ext-know) — [arXiv 2403.15879 (HTML v6)](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.15879v6); EHRSQL is one of TrustSQL's four datasets and is the origin of the penalty-scored reliability idea in the clinical setting (EHRSQL primary paper not fetched — UNVERIFIED detail)
- Few-run statistical practice (RL): "we advocate for reporting interval estimates of aggregate performance" with bootstrap-based confidence intervals, performance profiles for variability across runs, and "more robust and efficient aggregate metrics, such as interquartile mean scores", which have "small uncertainty with few runs"; implemented in the open-source rliable library; NeurIPS 2021 Outstanding Paper — [Agarwal et al., arXiv 2108.13264](https://arxiv.org/abs/2108.13264). (The specific phrase "stratified bootstrap" — resampling within each task/seed stratum — is in the full paper/library, not the abstract: UNVERIFIED at quote level.)
- Threshold-selection evaluation protocol actually used in the closest text-to-SQL study: thresholds chosen on a calibration split, risk/coverage reported on a held-out split, with the risk-coverage frontier as the headline (e.g., 27% coverage at 24% selective risk for the best signal; "no valid low-risk subset" for self-consistency) — [arXiv 2607.06799](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.06799)

### Inferences
- Recommended reporting set for the abstain-SQL paper, assembled from the above: (a) the full risk-coverage curve per method/signal; (b) AURC and E-AURC for threshold-free signal comparison; (c) realized selective risk and coverage at the certified threshold for each alpha in {0.05, 0.1, 0.2}, showing whether the guarantee held; (d) TrustSQL-style RS at c in {0, 10, N} for deployment-weighted comparison; (e) all point numbers with 95% bootstrap confidence intervals.
- Two variance sources need two resampling axes: test *items* (percentile bootstrap over queries, which captures benchmark-size noise and is the standard NLP practice) and training *seeds* (RL runs; use the Agarwal et al. machinery — IQM over seeds with bootstrap CIs — since 3-5 seeds is typical and the plain mean over so few runs is dominated by outliers). For comparing two training recipes, a paired bootstrap over items within each seed, then aggregation over seeds, is the defensible default. (Assembly is an inference; the item-level paired-bootstrap convention in NLP was not separately sourced this session — see Gaps.)
- Because conformal guarantees are *marginal* (Question 1), evaluation should also resample the *calibration split*: re-draw the calibration/test partition many times and report the distribution of realized risk around alpha — this is exactly what the CRC paper's own experiments do (1000 random splits, mean risk 0.0987 at alpha = 0.1).
